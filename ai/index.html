Scams that use AI

AI scams

Scammers now use AI to copy the voices of people you know, make videos of famous faces backing scams, and write convincing messages by the thousand. It's getting harder to spot from the message alone, so check who you're really dealing with before you pay or share anything.

How scammers use AI

  • Cloned voices

    A little audio found online is enough to copy someone's voice. Scammers use it to pose as a loved one in a crisis who needs money now, or as someone well known. People reported losing over $5 million to these "distress" calls in 2025.

    • Drill: The voicemail from a sheriff's captain
    • Drill: The friend calling from jail

  • Fake videos of famous faces

    Investment scams use AI-made videos and voices of celebrities, business leaders and other trusted figures, often as fake endorsements on social media. Investment complaints that mentioned AI reported over $632 million lost in 2025.

    • Drill: The finance expert's video
    • Investment scams →

  • Emails that sound like the boss

    AI writes official-sounding emails that copy a company's boss, and cloned voices can ask staff to send payments. Businesses reported losing over $30 million to these scams in 2025.

    • Drill: The urgent payment from the CEO
    • Business email compromise →

  • Fake people on video calls

    Scammers can appear on a live video call as a company boss, a police officer or another person in authority. They also make videos to "prove" that someone you've met online is real.

  • Fake profiles and chat scripts

    Scammers set up fake profiles with AI-made photos, and use scripts written by AI chatbots so their messages sound believable. Romance scams with a likely link to AI cost over $19 million in 2025.

  • No more spelling mistakes

    AI translates and polishes scam messages, so spelling mistakes and clumsy wording are no longer reliable warning signs.

Warning signs that still give it away

  • !Someone gets in touch out of the blue, even claiming to be a friend, relative or organisation you trust, and steers the conversation towards your personal details, bank or money.
  • !You're pushed to act right now, often over a crisis or a family emergency, with no time to check.
  • !They use fear or distress, or tell you to keep it secret, so you don't talk to anyone else.
  • !You're asked to pay by wire transfer, cryptocurrency or gift cards, which are hard to trace and almost impossible to get back.
  • !An online relationship gets intense very quickly, and they want to move the chat away from the dating site.

What to do

  • UK/USAgree a safe word or question with close family and friends, so you can check it's really them on a worrying call. Source: INTERPOL
  • UK/USIf someone calls out of the blue, even someone you know, hang up and contact them yourself on a number or account you know is theirs. Source: INTERPOL
  • UKWorried about your bank account? Call 159 to reach your own bank safely. Source: Stop Scams UK
  • UKLost money or shared your details? Report it to Report Fraud online or on 0300 123 2040. In Scotland, call Police Scotland on 101. Source: Stop! Think Fraud (UK Government)
  • USPaid a fake request? Contact your bank straight away to ask for a recall, and report it to the FBI at ic3.gov. Source: FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center
